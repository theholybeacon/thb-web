# Positioning (draft)

## The one-sentence read

The Holy Beacon is for people who already want to take the Bible seriously and
keep failing to do it consistently — and it competes not with other Bible apps
but with the reader's own good intentions.

## What the product's own copy claims

The competitive framing is unusually explicit and repeated, so I take it as
deliberate:

> "Most Bible apps hand you a verse and move on."
> "Not a verse-a-day app."

That is a positioning statement against a specific incumbent category — the
daily-verse, streak-and-inspiration app. The product is saying: that category
sells the *feeling* of engaging with Scripture; we sell actually working through
it.

---

## Target audience (inferred)

**Primary: the frustrated returner.** Someone with a real faith commitment who
has started reading the Bible several times and stalled. They do not need
motivating; they need structure, and a reason today's session is different from
the last three attempts. Every headline feature answers a stall:

| Where they stall | What answers it |
|---|---|
| "I don't know what to read next" | AI study plans, ready-made plans |
| "I read it and retain nothing" | Type mode |
| "I don't have time to sit and read" | Listen mode, background playback |
| "I don't understand who these people are" | Character profiles |
| "I lose everything I figure out" | Verse-anchored notes |
| "I can't tell if I'm getting anywhere" | The journey heatmap |

**Secondary: the curious reader with no account.** The entire Bible Explorer is
free and needs no sign-up, and the roadmap invests in shareable links and SEO.
This is a real acquisition channel, not a loss-leader afterthought — someone
lands on a chapter from a search or a shared link, and the product is good enough
that they stay.

**Tertiary: the Spanish-speaking reader.** Spanish is a fully translated
first-class interface, not a partial afterthought — 831 strings with no gaps. My
read is that this is a deliberate bet on an underserved market rather than a
courtesy.

**Who this is not for.** Casual "verse of the day" consumers — they are already
well served, and the product says so. Also not for academic scholars: the product
is honest that some of its original-language support is inference rather than
scholarship, so it is not competing with a critical apparatus.

---

## Differentiators, ranked by how defensible I think they are

**1. Type mode.** Nothing else meaningfully does this. It is strange enough to
stop a scroll, it demos in twenty seconds with no account and no setup, and it
carries a real idea — *slower, and that is exactly the point*. If one feature
does the marketing work, this is it. It is already the product's only built demo
video, which suggests you have reached the same conclusion.

**2. Three ways through the same chapter.** Not three features bolted together
but one chapter approached three ways, with progress that counts wherever you
started. That last detail is the hard part and the real moat.

**3. Cited character profiles.** "Every claim is cited to a real verse mention —
nothing invented" is a trust claim, and trust is the scarce commodity in
AI-assisted religious content. Worth leading with far more than a feature list
would suggest, because it is precisely the objection an informed reader has.

**4. The sponsorship program.** Nobody is turned away for not being able to pay,
and members can clear the queue for strangers. This is not a feature; it is
evidence of what the product is *for*. My instinct is that it belongs in brand
storytelling rather than in a feature ad — and that it is the single most
shareable true thing about the product.

**5. The free explorer.** 404 translations, no account. Strong for acquisition,
weaker as a differentiator — others do free reading too.

## Tone (inferred from the product's own writing)

Calm, plain, confident. Short sentences that state what a thing does. Reverent
about Scripture, unsentimental about itself.

The trait I would protect most is **honesty about limits**. The product tells a
reader on screen when an original-language match is a model's guess rather than
scholarship. It refuses to narrate copyrighted translations and says why. Almost
nothing in this category does that. I think it is a genuine brand asset and the
marketing should inherit it — never promising audio that might not exist, never
implying certainty the product doesn't have.

**What I'd avoid:** guilt ("when did you last open your Bible?"), hype
("revolutionary"), and any hint of theological authority. The product presents
and cites; it does not interpret for you. Copy that starts interpreting breaks
the promise.

## Content angles I'd test

**Type mode as the hook.** Psalm 117 typed on camera in twenty seconds, live
accuracy and WPM, ending on the stat tiles. Public, no auth, no audio, no AI —
it films with nothing to arrange. Existing hook: *"You don't read Scripture. You
type it."*

**"Who is this person?"** Open on a name buried in a genealogy, then their
profile with dates, timeline and citations. Sells the character library and the
citation discipline in one move.

**The heatmap reveal.** 1,189 squares filling in. Progress as an image, and the
product already generates a vertical share card for exactly this.

**Verse of the day, done properly.** The daily verse endpoint is deterministic
and shared by every reader, so a daily post says the same thing the app says.
This is the obvious daily cadence — but it is also the closest thing to the
verse-a-day category the product positions against, so I would frame it as an
entry point rather than as the product.

**The sponsorship story.** Least like an ad, most likely to travel.

---

## Where I think the risk is

**The free tier is generous enough to be the whole product for many people.**
404 translations, character pages, progress tracking and reading mode, all free
forever. The conversion argument rests almost entirely on study plans, audio and
notes. I do not know whether that is a deliberate acquisition strategy or an
accident of what shipped first — and the answer changes what marketing should
lead with.

**Coverage is uneven.** Not every chapter of every translation is loaded, and
narration exists only where it has been produced. Campaign passages need
checking, not assuming.
